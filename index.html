Introduction to Electrocardiography

An electrocardiogram (ECG or EKG) is a non-invasive test that records the electrical activity of the heart over time.

Learning Objectives

  • Understand what an ECG measures.
  • Identify the basic components of an ECG tracing.
  • Understand the clinical importance of ECG interpretation.

What does an ECG measure?

The ECG records electrical impulses generated by cardiac depolarization and repolarization. It does not directly measure the mechanical contraction of the heart.

Clinical Uses

  • Detection of cardiac arrhythmias.
  • Evaluation of myocardial ischemia and infarction.
  • Detection of conduction abnormalities.
  • Assessment of electrolyte-related ECG changes.

Important Note

An ECG must always be interpreted alongside the patient's symptoms, clinical examination, and medical history.

Cardiac Anatomy and Electrical Conduction

The heart contains four chambers: two atria and two ventricles.

The Cardiac Conduction System

  1. SA Node: The normal primary pacemaker of the heart.
  2. AV Node: Delays electrical conduction before ventricular activation.
  3. Bundle of His: Conducts impulses into the ventricular conduction system.
  4. Right and Left Bundle Branches: Carry impulses toward the ventricles.
  5. Purkinje Fibers: Distribute impulses throughout ventricular myocardium.

Normal Electrical Sequence

SA node → Atrial myocardium → AV node → Bundle of His → Bundle branches → Purkinje fibers.

Clinical Significance

Abnormalities in any part of the conduction system may produce characteristic ECG changes, including bradycardia, heart block, and bundle branch block.

ECG Waves, Segments and Intervals

P Wave

Represents atrial depolarization.

PR Interval

Represents conduction from the beginning of atrial depolarization to the beginning of ventricular depolarization. Normal duration: 120–200 ms.

QRS Complex

Represents ventricular depolarization. Normal duration is generally less than 120 ms.

ST Segment

Extends from the end of the QRS complex to the beginning of the T wave. ST elevation or depression may indicate myocardial injury or ischemia in the appropriate clinical context.

T Wave

Represents ventricular repolarization.

QT Interval

Represents the duration of ventricular depolarization and repolarization. QT interpretation should account for heart rate using QT correction (QTc).

Heart Rate and Rhythm Analysis

Calculating Heart Rate

For a regular rhythm using standard paper speed of 25 mm/s:

Heart Rate = 300 ÷ Number of large squares between R waves.

Example

If there are 4 large squares between two consecutive R waves:

Heart Rate = 300 ÷ 4 = 75 beats/min.

Irregular Rhythms

For a 10-second ECG strip, count the QRS complexes and multiply by 6 to estimate the ventricular rate.

Rhythm Assessment

  • Is the rhythm regular or irregular?
  • Are P waves present?
  • Does every P wave precede a QRS complex?
  • Is the PR interval constant?
  • Is the QRS duration normal?

Normal sinus rhythm originates from the sinoatrial node and follows the normal cardiac conduction pathway.
